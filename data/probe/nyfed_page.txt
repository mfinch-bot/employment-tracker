September Survey: Inflation Expectations Up at Short- and Medium-Term, Unchanged at Longer-Term Horizons; Labor Market Expectations <br>Mostly Improve                </div>

                <ul style="list-style: outside; padding-left: 1em;">
    <li style="list-style: outside; padding-left: 1em;">Median inflation expectations increased by 0.3&nbsp;percentage point (ppt) at the one-year horizon and by 0.1&nbsp;ppt at the three-year horizon, to 3.9&nbsp;percent and 3.3&nbsp;percent, respectively. This is the highest reading for one-year-ahead inflation expectations since May&nbsp;2023. Expectations remained unchanged at the five-year-ahead horizon at 3.0&nbsp;percent. </li>
<li style="list-style: outside; padding-left: 1em;">Mean unemployment expectations—or the mean probability that the U.S. unemployment rate will be higher one year from now—decreased by 0.5&nbsp;ppt to 43.9&nbsp;percent but remained above the 12-month trailing average of 42.4&nbsp;percent. The mean perceived probability of losing one’s job in the next twelve months decreased by 0.3&nbsp;ppt to 13.5&nbsp;percent, the lowest reading since December&nbsp;2024. </li>
<li style="list-style: outside; padding-left: 1em;">The median expected growth in household income increased by 0.1&nbsp;ppt to 3.1&nbsp;percent, the highest reading since February&nbsp;2025. Median one-year-ahead nominal household spending growth expectations increased by 0.3&nbsp;ppt to 5.5&nbsp;percent, above the 12-month trailing average of 5.0&nbsp;percent and the highest reading of the series since May&nbsp;2023. </li>
</li>
<li style="list-style: outside; padding-left: 1em;">Perceptions and expectations about households’ financial situations both deteriorated, with larger shares of households reporting a worse financial situation compared to a year ago and expecting a worse financial situation a year from now. </li>
 
</ul>

<div class="mainAccordionContent">
    <div class="ts-contact-info-headline" style="margin-top:20px">Fielding the Survey</div>
    <div class="ts-accordion-content ">
        The SCE is a nationally representative, Internet-based survey of a rotating panel of approximately 1,300 household heads. Respondents participate in the panel for up to twelve months, with a roughly equal number rotating in and out of the panel each month. Unlike comparable surveys based on repeated cross-sections with a different set of respondents in each wave, our panel enables us to observe the changes in expectations and behavior of the same individuals over time.
        <div class="paragraph-more">
            <div class="viewbutton" style="display: block;">
                <button style="margin-top:5px" class="form_button" id="showmore">READ MORE</button>
            </div>
            <div id="hidden-text" class="ts-accordion-content" style="display: none;">
                <br>
                The survey is conducted on our behalf by The Demand Institute, a nonprofit organization jointly operated by The Conference Board and Nielsen. The sampling frame for the SCE is based on that used for The Conference Board&rsquo;s Consumer Confidence Survey (CCS). Respondents to the CCS, itself based on a representative national sample drawn from mailing addresses, are invited to join the SCE internet panel.<br />
<br />
The SCE, which launched in 2013, focuses primarily on expectations about economic outcomes related to inflation, the labor market, and household finance. In eliciting subjective expectations for certain variables, such as future inflation, we collect data on the individual&rsquo;s forecast uncertainty. The survey tracks a respondent&rsquo;s age, income, education, homeownership status, employment history, and region; it also tests for level of numeracy. A November 2016 New York Fed <a href="/research/staff_reports/sr800"><strong>staff report</strong></a> offers an overview of the survey methodology. We summarize the main survey results in a monthly New York Fed press release, illustrate trends in a series of interactive graphics on the CMD website, and publish a range of analyses. The data are available to other researchers, policymakers, and the public.
